# The Acceptable Error — Story Lab 02: The House of Tomorrow

**Date:** 2026-11-07  
**Status:** Exploratory story concept for owner review; no art, scene, plot, or implementation lock  
**Role:** First potential post-reception adventure in **The Exposition of Everything**  
**Continuity:** [60 — Exposition Opening and Story Engine](60_EXPOSITION_OPENING_AND_STORY_ENGINE_V01.md)  
**Boundary:** No changes to current reception M2 implementation, layout, sprites, runtime, or local code.

## Executive story pitch

**Logline:** Just after being accidentally assigned authority at the Exposition, an unemployed nobody becomes the involuntary resident of an immaculate automated home. The home promises an effortless life but requires its sole resident to complete an endless series of procedures to certify that no effort is required. During the demonstration, the entire model home physically moves, forcing the resident onward into another pavilion.

**Core comic contradiction:** The House of Tomorrow automates every task by generating more work for the person it claims to serve. Its machinery is elaborate, mostly analog and physically plausible within retro-futurist pulp conventions; no evil AI reveal.

**A good first chapter should teach that the player doesn't control whether an absurd institution happens to them, but does control their response, what they discover, what they retain, and what happens to others.** Choices can matter even when the story progresses forward.

## What is already owner-established vs what is proposed

### OWNER-ESTABLISHED FRAME (from docs 60 and current conversation)

- The Exposition of Everything is the larger adventure setting, with distinct rooms/floors/buildings/pavilions that can seem like different worlds.
- The protagonist was unfairly fired for an absurd clerical accident, went searching for a bathroom, lost most belongings at Expo entry, and became bureaucratically misassigned at reception.
- The adventure is choice-first, substantially forward-moving, with occasional humorous allegories and escalating oddness.
- The bathroom is a *starting motive*, not a permanent needs meter or incessant joke. Later callbacks should be rare.
- The other PMO and Claude are independently finishing the reception slice. This story lab is conceptual only.

### PROPOSED FOR OWNER REVIEW

- Making the model house a rotating/movable exhibition set; the public entrance disappears due to stage motion, rather than a suspiciously convenient lock.
- A live accelerated household-demonstration schedule: morning, lunch, leisure, night, all compressed to a short exhibition.
- A house "hospitality" system that automates tasks by creating mandatory forms, verifications and labor.
- One recurring human technician and one fellow demonstration participant, with most information presented environmentally rather than by dialogue trees.
- A final movement into **The Transport Miracle** pavilion via an automated house relocation demonstration.
- One gently uncanny physical discrepancy that can be unrelated, misinterpreted, or connected to later mystery, but does **not** prove the Exchange.
- Approximate story structure below. Neither its number of scenes nor its chapter title is final.

## 1. Opening handoff from reception

The receptionist's established mistaken appointment gives some form of demonstration eligibility, access or instruction. **Do not mandate specific new dialogue or a new job title in M2**. For example, a generic note or pass might point the protagonist toward a demonstration home where visitor facilities are available.

The House advertises:

> THE HOUSE OF TOMORROW  
> NO WAITING. NO WORK. NO WORRIES.  
> REST FACILITIES PROVIDED WITH EVERY MODEL.

The protagonist sees a bathroom icon on the floor plan. Whether this is a functioning bathroom is *not* the main mystery; it serves as one brief early lure and joke. The scene immediately has reasons to move beyond it.

## 2. Visual identity and rules of the place

**Exterior:** A shining suburban prefab home in an immense exhibition hall, framed by brilliant painted skies, glass, planted plastic shrubs and an artificial white-picket fence. Better maintained than the place where the protagonist was just fired.

**Interior:** Rounded enamel panels, large colored mechanical controls, reel-to-reel scheduling machinery, conveyor pantry, pneumatic forms, furniture that slides on floor tracks, pleasantly absurd product names, intercom announcements with cheerful prerecorded voice. A few visible repairs make the world feel lived in. Avoid generic sleek screens, brass-and-rivet steampunk or an omnipotent digital assistant.

**Physical principle:** The entire house is a giant functioning exhibit built on a rotating and subsequently mobile stage. The official demonstration runs forward through predetermined daily phases. Each interior room changes function as partitions, props and machinery shift. It isn't magical or malicious: it is an undermaintained system used in ways its designers no longer question.

**Social principle:** Human operators keep the demonstration running, but its advertising describes zero labor. Staff know the absurdity. Most simply regard it as what a good demonstration requires.

## 3. Character cast — minimum necessary

**THE HOUSE / THE VOICE OF COMFORT (non-person):** A recorded, warmly cheerful announcer. It speaks in short preset notices and congratulates residents for having chosen things they never chose. This is NOT an AI antagonist or a conversation NPC. More effective when it cannot engage in argument.

**MARA VALE / “THE NEIGHBOR” (provisional character, name not locked):** An apparently cheerful resident of the identical demonstration house visible through a window. She has participated so many times that she knows the demonstration's rituals better than staff. Her public persona is relentlessly upbeat, but privately she may warn visitors about the closing movement or quietly help them. An ordinary person with motives of her own, not a quirky exposition-dump NPC. Alternative: a new visitor who arrives by mistake, preventing an experienced-insider shortcut.

**ADA PINN / MAINTENANCE TECHNICIAN (provisional):** A practical employee who manually resets household modules through narrow service openings. She sincerely likes the machinery and believes the Exposition is worth preserving, but knows how much human effort hides behind “effortless living.” May supply useful information or a minor object. She should be competent and neither omniscient nor secretly leading the mystery.

**PUBLIC HOST (optional):** A single smiling human emcee can appear in the exterior introduction or a short conclusion. Avoid adding five NPC conversations; the house and its procedures should carry the story.

**Character caution:** Don't commit to gender, name, age, backstory, or eventual recurring importance. One human companion might be enough; the two supporting roles can merge if narrative economy calls for it.

## 4. Suggested sequence of authored incidents

### BEAT A — WELCOME TO YOUR LIFE

The player enters a pleasant model home, motivated briefly by the public bathroom sign. A telephone announces the beginning of the domestic tour. The house's instruction panel requires its resident to confirm that they are "relaxed" and "free from obligations."

As the protagonist looks back, the outside doorway has become an artificial garden wall because the entire stage rotated. The tour is already in motion.

**Story purpose:** Capture the feeling of unavoidable forward movement without arbitrarily locking a normal door. Show the first environmental close-up: an impossibly detailed household schedule.

**Possible choices, conceptually:** participate, try the front entrance, study schedule, inspect odd equipment. Different reactions produce knowledge, an item or attention, even though the demonstration begins regardless.

### BEAT B — THE AUTOMATED BREAKFAST

A kitchen makes a beautiful breakfast without anyone touching a pan. Unfortunately, it first asks the resident to classify appetite, justify food allocation, sign a temperature acceptance notice, count unused utensils and certify that meal preparation required no labor.

The meal arrives when the subject's registered mealtime has already ended. The kitchen efficiently disposes of it and generates a receipt showing one meal enjoyed.

**Story purpose:** Pure comedy with tangible choices; no important mystery clue necessary.

**Promising choice tension:** Eat something that wasn't authorized, obtain a useful utensil, interrupt disposal, help a staffer trapped behind the conveyor or sign the report. The player cannot do everything.

### BEAT C — THE SELF-CLEANING LIVING ROOM

A robotic vacuum emerges from an enamel baseboard, then multiple furniture-moving appliances coordinate to clean the room. Their system insists the resident remain inside a precisely marked *clean area*, which shrinks as furniture slides.

A retractable chair almost crushes a display plant or a worker's hand behind the panel. Staff call the event a "minimum-contact housekeeping demonstration."

**Story purpose:** First action-oriented scene; environment rather than NPC chat; first physical stakes are modest and absurd. Someone may genuinely benefit from the player's decision.

**Potential consequence:** Help the maintenance worker, save an object, evade automated relocation, allow it to complete as designed. No numeric roll formula decided yet.

### BEAT D — THE NEIGHBORHOOD EXPERIENCE

Curtains rise to reveal a fully artificial suburban street. Through one window a neighbor waves, then waves again at precisely the same time as before. A recorded voice announces the house has achieved "social enrichment" because the neighbor engagement routine completed successfully.

If the protagonist signals differently, the neighbor may break character and convey practical information, possibly through a handwritten placard or an object rather than a long conversation.

**Story purpose:** Small human relationship; a slight crack between public performance and lived reality. Optional discovery about how carefully the whole exhibit is controlled.

**Alternate version:** The neighbor isn't an employee; she's another trapped visitor. This changes the ethics and tone, so test as a story choice later.

### BEAT E — THE MOST CONVENIENT ROOM

The household announces its final feature: **domestic relief**. The former restroom is now a "fully autonomous sanitation lifecycle" demonstration. Its actual private room was removed for efficiency because the system's new brochure says residents need not visit it.

This should be the **one** bathroom callback within the adventure. Play the disappointment once, sharply; then move on to a bigger immediate problem.

A service grate reveals the machinery behind the promise, where an exhausted worker or small imperiled object needs attention. The brochure says: "All domestic work is automatically completed." The player has seen precisely who or what completes it.

**Story purpose:** Transform the joke into an observation: convenience doesn't necessarily remove work; it may move it elsewhere. This can echo the game's overarching theme without committing to any scientific mechanism.

### BEAT F — RELOCATION IS A HOME FEATURE

The house completes its day. The recorded voice announces:

> "Your home is now ready to accompany you wherever you wish to go."

Heavy latches release. The floor vibrates. Exterior walls slide aside. The protagonist discovers the complete model house is mounted on a freight rail platform and is already being shipped to the next pavilion for a **portable housing demonstration**.

The protagonist can try to find an exit, help the human operators, grab a useful item, or seek a safer position before movement. The home is too large to stop by argument; its movement is an institutional schedule, not a villain's scheme.

**Story purpose:** A cinematic forward-only transition that physically demonstrates the Exposition's interconnected, purpose-built worlds.

## 5. The end should feel like an earned escalation

**RECOMMENDED TRANSITION: INTO THE TRANSPORT MIRACLE**

The living room sets are lifted away as the prefab house travels down immense industrial tracks. The morning-sun backdrop collapses behind it, revealing a vast transport terminal full of suspended cargo, demonstration trains and model vehicles. The protagonist has been delivered from “comfort” into a grand display of movement.

The new pavilion could continue with a narrator line along the lines of:

> I had been promised a home that went wherever I wanted. The home had selected where it wanted to go.

This transition makes **architecture the punchline** and opens new landscapes without a dialogue-only assignment. It's a natural visual bridge to a future Transit/Transport story, but **does not lock the next chapter or the Exchange mechanism**.

**Alternative B — EMERGENCY EXPERIENCE:** A domestic safety routine declares a harmless issue an emergency and diverts the home into a public disaster-drill pavilion. More action and faster escalation; risk of turning early comedy into real peril too soon.

**Alternative C — SERVICE UNDERSTRUCTURE:** The protagonist slips through maintenance access into the hidden skeleton of the Expo and emerges at a radically different attraction. Rewards curiosity; potentially undercuts spectacle if our first two venues are both back-of-house corridors.

**Recommended:** A as default dramatic route, with B or C potentially informing route variations *after* story structure is chosen. Don't build a multi-branch graph yet.

## 6. What can actually change through choices? (without drafting dice)

**The main sequence remains:** Enter model home → obligations accumulate → discover invisible labor/neighbor ritual → home relocates → new location.

But choices should affect:
- Whether the player recognizes the house's manual mode or service corridors.
- Whether the neighbor trusts/helps them later, if that character recurs.
- Whether they obtain a domestic tool, paper receipt or facility access token.
- Whether they cause a mild injury, prevent one, or get officially blamed for one.
- Whether the technician gives them a practical tip or regards them as another careless inspector.
- Which part of the Transport Pavilion they arrive at and what they are carrying/known for.

**Limit scope:** Three to five consequential decisions across the episode may be plenty; avoid constant inconsequential prompts. Some comedic setpieces are better experienced as narrated moments.

## 7. Gadget/oddity catalogue (pick, don't stack)

1. **The Comfort Certificate:** House announces that 100% of residents experienced complete relaxation, provided the resident signs one last form.
2. **The Automatic Apology Slot:** An appliance apologizes in advance for failures it will cause to meet its quota of apologies successfully issued.
3. **The Pre-Cooled Soup Bowl:** The kitchen anticipates a meal so thoroughly it has disposed of the meal by the time it arrives.
4. **Furniture With Appointment Times:** A recliner can be used only within its scheduled relaxation interval; it then retracts into a wall while occupied.
5. **The Courtesy Neighbor:** A curtain reveals a precisely timed wave from the neighboring demonstration house, but the neighbor may actually have agency.
6. **The Pneumatic Lost-Purpose Registry:** Every retired appliance is tagged as still operational because it remains capable of producing a completed certificate.
7. **Automatic Dust Relocation:** Vacuum system moves dust through tubes into another part of the pavilion and reports the house perfectly clean.
8. **The Morning Reset:** A recorded sunrise replays when one procedure runs late, making it appear that the demonstration has completed twice.
9. **Three Exit Doors, One Freight Platform:** Different “exit” signs lead to different parts of the same moving home; their promises are technically accurate, not useful.
10. **The Sanitation Lifecycle:** Brochure celebrates freedom from daily necessities while omitting where physical waste and the human labor actually go.

**Rule:** Select two or three memorable machines to carry the episode rather than a barrage of disconnected gags. Every major scene must alter the predicament or change how it is understood.

## 8. Allegory, mystery and emotional handling

**Allegorical premise:** Productivity/automation often doesn't abolish labor; it hides, transfers, or reclassifies labor and then produces measurements declaring success. The protagonist's firing and subsequent employment-like appointment echo this.

**Early tone:** Bright, funny, invitingly weird. Things are odd but people treat them normally.

**Middle tone:** The design's claims and operations contradict. A person is affected. The player can make a small real difference.

**Uncanny whisper, OPTIONAL:** On a prop storage shelf, two identical household items carry serial records that can't both be correct, or a room is laid out differently than a supposedly exact model diagram. Treat as ambiguous: sloppy paperwork and strange machinery are plausible explanations. This is **not** confirmation of The Exchange or a secret technology reveal.

**Emotional anchor:** One decent worker or resident is more important than another elaborate prank. The first Expo chapter should establish that behind ridiculous procedures are actual people worth caring about.

**Avoid:** Evil sentient smart-home plot, a repetitive physical prison, recycling the bathroom joke in every room, full escape-room point-and-click play, generic AI dialogue, constant death danger, fifteen novelty devices with no dramatic arc, and explaining the overarching mystery in the first pavilion.

## 9. Potential scene visuals (for story readability, not production requests)

- A bright house at the back of a huge exhibit hall; bathroom floor-plan icon visible.
- A modular living room with cheery 1960s finishes, analog scheduling wall and disappearing entry.
- A conveyor kitchen, lovely food, monstrous document printer.
- A moving living room with furniture on tracks and a shrinking clean zone.
- An artificial neighborhood behind a curtain with an occupied second house.
- Narrow maintenance area behind enamel panels (short detail, not a new walkaround level).
- Final wide reveal: the entire house rolling into a cavernous transport pavilion.

These support the established coarse world-scene / finer close-up distinction without dictating UI implementation. Not all require unique production assets; content scope will be assessed later.

## 10. What we should decide next

**Primary owner reaction requested:** Does “automation creates hidden domestic labor” feel sufficiently original, funny, and consistent with the world's offness? Is the chapter *too much* of a forced residency story, or a good example of forward-only CYOA?

**Most useful creative choices:**
- **A — Character first:** Develop the neighbor and practical technician as compelling humans, possibly merge them.
- **B — Wildness first:** Try bolder, less familiar room/house concepts: different physical realities, rotating artificial neighborhoods, unsettling model families, strange rules. Compare with this more grounded satire.
- **C — Transition first:** Deepen the house-relocation climax and design how the Transport Pavilion becomes the second adventure.
- **D — Opening prose:** Outline the first 3–4 incident beats with sample narrative/choices but do not write a fully scripted chapter yet.

**PMO recommendation:** B next, followed by A and C. We want the first Expo exhibit to demonstrate the surprising variety promised by the larger setting, not merely be a generic smart home with malfunctioning appliances.
