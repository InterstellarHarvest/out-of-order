# World and story laboratory
Status: exploratory; none of the following is final story canon.

## World: a past generation's promised future
A 1960s-style imaginary future: monumental exhibition architecture, curving monorails, brushed metal, thick glass, analog computers, reel-to-reel machinery, intercoms, mechanical relays, pneumatic paperwork, public science pavilions, advanced synthetic biology, and government/corporate oversight. The technology can be wondrous and truly functional; facilities may be run-down and administrative processes contradictory. Think the satirical institutional mood of *Brazil*, with room for pulpy expedition and creature encounters in the spirit of *Jurassic Park*, without reproducing their plots.

'Meridian' was proposed as a demonstration city that outgrew its temporary world's-fair origins. Its research/exhibition complex, company neighborhoods, experimental preserves, forgotten service networks, and possibly distant outposts are optional geography—not a committed map or final name. Resist trapping the entire adventure inside one laboratory.

## The player: a nobody with unsuitable credentials
An ordinary individual arrives on a mundane errand. A clerical or identity mix-up labels them a special examiner / inspector / appointee with inappropriate access. Attempts to correct the record collide with procedures; the fiction should not quietly recast the protagonist as an expert or chosen one. Why the misappointment happened is **open**: pure coincidence, deliberate manipulation, or a deeper implication are all possibilities, with genuine coincidence preferred over an easy destiny reveal.

Earlier prototype opening: a receptionist declares that the player is the appointed 'Special Examiner, Office of Unverified Conditions'; protests are treated as an administrative difficulty rather than grounds for release. The title, office, and exact dialogue are placeholders.

## Proposed opening motive: the bathroom detour (owner preference, 2026-10-29)
The protagonist is an ordinary person with an ordinary bodily need: after **a couple of earlier, unrelated scenes/events**, they enter the facility **only to ask where the bathroom is**. The receptionist somehow converts this innocent request into a mistaken official role assignment through bureaucratic nonsense. The prior scenes don't belong in the initial M1 demo—start the visual comparison at reception.

The protagonist's repeated inability to actually use a bathroom may become a **running gag**: new paperwork, diversions, assignments, mistaken authority, and unexpected crises constantly intrude. Tentative flavor, not locked comic frequency. Consider keeping the initial innocent purpose alive without relying on a literal bodily-needs timer or turning every scene into the same joke. No special destiny or hidden qualifications are implied.

## Leading mystery: The Exchange
Humanity perfected a ubiquitous system publicly understood as near-instant transport of matter. It underlies logistics, manufacturing, supply chains, medicine, passenger travel, and possibly the visible optimism of modern life. Reliable outcomes seem to confirm the prevailing explanation.

**Candidate true mechanism:** rather than moving an original object from A to B, the machine identifies an equivalent counterpart elsewhere and performs an exchange. Some differences may be negligible for bulk material. For living things, memories, biological traits, and identity, 'equivalent' is more unsettling.

**Possible signs**, not yet a plot:
- A returned personal possession bears a tiny change with no plausible manufacturing explanation.
- A traveler recalls an event that other records deny occurred.
- A cargo manifest is locally correct but conflicts with an inventory someplace no one can name.
- A wildlife enclosure or biological specimen has a history more extensive than the facility housing it.
- The institution lists every transfer as successful, but an obscure balancing metric is drifting.
- A 'service' terminal or discarded paper manual describes the mechanism more accurately than the official theory.

**Open rules we must settle before plotting:** What is exchanged, with what, and under which constraints? Are originals somewhere else? Can subjects be recovered? Are differences truly subtle or sometimes overt? What limits substitution? Is there intent or only a physical process? How does the system fail? What must remain unknown to preserve tension while still making the story fair? Do not assume a multiverse, aliens, time travel, or consciousness transfer without selecting it.

## Other mystery mechanisms, kept alive
- **The Margin:** variation or defects apparently eliminated by precision technology are displaced elsewhere, shifting hidden costs onto others.
- **The Forecast:** a predictive system increases the likelihood of predicted events, gradually suppressing alternatives and free choices.
- **The Observer Effect:** a measured phenomenon's behavior is partially established or maintained by repeated experimental procedures rather than simply observed.
- **Cost/standardization version:** later generations scaled a genuine discovery beyond the conditions for which its behavior was understood.

These are alternative truths, potential thematic motifs, or red herrings—not stacked explanations.

## Narrative shape (possible)
A braided investigation: small local adventures and optional evidence routes feed several shared dramatic events. Choices, acquired objects, relationships, and failed attempts alter how the player reaches them. An absurd initial assignment becomes a chance to compare observations other departments keep apart. Later the protagonist has disproportionate authority but no matching expertise; the climax forces an action with incomplete evidence rather than an omniscient final quiz.

The mood progression is **comic inconvenience → escalating inconsistency → disturbing consequence → uneasy choice**. It should not be a rigid four-act script.

## Provisional people, not a cast lock
- **Miss Voss**, an impeccably procedural receptionist whose knowledge of forgotten rules may matter.
- **Dr. Elias Rook**, careful and capable at experiments but vulnerable to overinterpretation or obsession.
- **Marta Bell**, practical maintenance technician with material knowledge and reason to keep secrets.
- **Director Havel**, cordial administrator who may sincerely believe public confidence protects lives.
- **Penny Vale**, cheerful public-demonstrations guide whose apparent certainty may shift with context.
They are option sketches. Change or discard names and roles freely. Let at least some professionals be admirable, not all corrupt fools.

## Story risks
Avoid 'everything was a simulation/dream', 'evil computer did it', the secretly predestined hero, false mysteries whose solutions violate established rules, or a revelation that nullifies player decisions. Some incidents should remain independent. Keep humor and beauty alongside dread.

## Story questions to investigate later
What was the original discovery? What makes this world distinct from ours beyond retro aesthetics? What tangible problem starts the first adventure? Why does the protagonist stay involved after discovering the mistake? What does a 'successful' ending mean if shutting the Exchange down harms millions? Which early comic detail can be reinterpreted later without cheating?
