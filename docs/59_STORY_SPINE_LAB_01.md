## What comes from the older record

- The player is the **completely wrong person**: unexceptional, not secretly chosen or qualified.
- A few unrelated earlier incidents may occur before the player enters the institutional complex solely to ask for a **bathroom**. M2's playable reception demo intentionally starts at reception; the full story may start earlier.
- The receptionist turns a simple question into an incorrect official appointment. Repeated efforts to find a washroom or correct the mistake instead cause more bureaucracy. The bathroom is a running comic motive, **not a survival timer**.
- Alternate retro-futuristic public society is as important as the offices: 1960s-imagined future, exhibitions, transit, neighborhoods, experimental wildlife, infrastructure, scientists and maintenance staff.
- Normal citizens treat very strange systems/procedures as routine. Institutional logic should often be internally consistent, albeit distorted by accumulated absurd rules. The joke is not “everyone is stupid.”
- Emotional trajectory: **comic inconvenience → widening absurdity → contradiction → disturbing consequences → difficult choice**. Humor, beauty and unrelated oddities persist.
- Central thematic mystery: functional modern civilization may misunderstand the real workings of its impressive technology. **The Exchange** is a strong candidate but not final canon. Alternatives The Margin, Forecast, Observer Effect and scale-up remain live.
- Preserved incident seeds: an exhibit files a complaint, a specimen is older than its enclosure, records describe future incidents, and contradictory organizational charts hide an authority. These are *not* already approved chapters.
- Gameplay remains House of Danger-like CYOA, with meaningful items, hidden dice/checks for uncertain attempts, and consequential divergence; implementation details remain for later.

## Immediate creative problem: why does the protagonist keep going?

A bathroom errand is an excellent **inciting motive**, but by itself does not credibly sustain an entire adventure after leaving the building becomes possible.

Possible **motive ladder** (not locked; multiple rungs may occur):
1. **Immediate discomfort:** find the washroom.
2. **Unwanted obligation:** cancel the official misassignment; perhaps can't access mundane services until a mistake is corrected.
3. **Personal jeopardy:** an official record/credential begins affecting where the protagonist may go, what services they receive, or whether others recognize them.
4. **Moral involvement:** a human being, creature, or working community suffers because an apparently routine error was classified as “acceptable.”
5. **Choice with consequence:** the protagonist is in an unusual position to sign, refuse, reveal or redirect a process they only partly understand.

The protagonist must be free to make believable attempts to walk away. The story should show the consequences and practical obstacles rather than simply disabling LEAVE. They may begin by wanting to escape and later continue through curiosity, loyalty, guilt, self-protection or concern for others. The player's choices should influence **why** they continue. If some paths terminate early, those are legitimate outcomes worth considering later.

## Three story layers — potential elements of one coherent adventure

### Layer A — The Bureaucratic Nightmare

**Story engine:** Every attempt to fulfill a simple need or correct an identity error generates a new responsibility.

**Scenes that fit:** public reception, restroom entitlement counter, mismatched credentials, a corridor where different departments disagree on what counts as an entrance, a scheduling office that does not acknowledge unplanned exits, a records office that generates a job title rather than correcting it.

**Unique comedic promise:** The protagonist starts with no authority and becomes more institutionally powerful each time they try to disclaim their authority.

**Dramatic danger:** Administrative categories could eventually affect real people, not just forms. A signature wrongly applied may authorize a genuinely harmful act.

**Risk:** If this is the entire game, we get one conversation with a clerk after another. It needs environmental incidents, travel and tangible consequences.

### Layer B — The World Beyond the Office

**Story engine:** A fictional assignment the protagonist never asked for sends them through wonderfully strange parts of a functioning society.

**Scenes that fit:** crowded exhibition pavilion, automated showcase, monorail or cargo transfer hub, maintenance tunnels, public demonstration that goes sideways, company neighborhood, experimental wildlife preserve, overnight infrastructure service, industrial greenhouse or remote outpost.

**Unique adventure promise:** Travel, peril, unusual equipment, documents, chance and discoveries emerge organically from the wrongful assignment.

**Dramatic danger:** The protagonist gradually learns that otherwise separate departments explain incompatible facts in mutually convincing ways.

**Risk:** Without an evolving personal motive and mystery, these become disconnected satirical skits. Each important location must do character, plot or revelation work.

### Layer C — Something Is Fundamentally Wrong

**Story engine:** Small apparent inconsistencies become evidence of a serious mismatch between accepted theory and observed reality.

**Early signs (subtle):** paperwork that does not match a machine's output; a transported object with a tiny change; a person who remembers a different version of a public event; a specimen whose recorded past predates its facility.

**Middle:** Competing explanations that each seem plausible; scientists disagree from good faith and limited evidence; administrators protect ordinary people from panic or protect their own status, and sometimes both.

**Later:** A practical decision is forced before the full truth is known. The wrong person holds a seal, form, access right or witness testimony that matters.

**Unique mystery promise:** The absurdity is slowly reinterpreted, not instantly reduced to a conspiracy or evil computer.

**Risk:** Unifying every joke under a master explanation destroys the texture of the world. Some absurdities must remain unrelated and some ordinary people remain genuinely good at their jobs.

**Recommended blend to explore:** Layer A launches the plot, Layer B supplies the adventures and variety, Layer C emerges through patterns and consequences. This is a recommendation, **not owner-approved canon**.

## Three macro-story proposals to compare

These are story *structures* rather than branching graphs.

### PITCH 1 — The Unwanted Inspection (strongest continuity with earlier archive)

**One-sentence version:** A bathroom seeker is accidentally credentialed to inspect a public scientific institution and keeps discovering discrepancies that each department explains away by making him responsible for the next one.

**Middle story:** He is sent to an exhibit that has filed a complaint; then to a facility whose records contradict the exhibit; then into maintenance spaces where systems are actually kept operating. Other witnesses may be allies, antagonists, skeptical professionals or accidental guides.

**Possible late dilemma:** Approving an “acceptable discrepancy” keeps a vital civic system running but hides uncertainty or harm; refusing may shut down services innocent people depend on.

**Strength:** Natural progression from the reception mistake, recurring comedy, title relevance, multiple locations and slow unease.

**Weakness:** Could feel like an episodic detective checklist; avoid making every destination a “collect clue and interview NPC” case.

### PITCH 2 — The Exposition of Everything

**One-sentence version:** A visitor seeking a bathroom is mistaken for a surprise evaluator just as a citywide public celebration of its future technologies is being prepared.

**Middle story:** The player is sent to spectacular demonstration sites and public venues. Differences between what the exhibits *say* and what the world *does* begin appearing. A malfunctioning machine may produce something more frightening than an equipment failure. The city is worth seeing independently of plot secrets.

**Possible late dilemma:** Proceed with a major public launch, interrupt it with incomplete evidence, or quietly modify it while exposing different people to risk.

**Strength:** Spectacle, humor, wonder, regional variety, set-piece opportunities, very gameable choice situations.

**Weakness:** More assets and a danger of world-spanning stakes too early. A scheduled event should not force a rigid countdown through the entire game.

### PITCH 3 — An Error in the Person

**One-sentence version:** The bureaucracy's mistaken records begin to affect the protagonist's actual identity and the memories of people nearby, turning a harmless administrative error into a personal mystery.

**Middle story:** Credentials, surveillance and human witnesses tell conflicting stories. The protagonist may be granted access, denied a service, mistaken for someone else or confronted with a duplicate record. This could fit the Exchange or other mystery models—but no explanation is yet assumed.

**Possible late dilemma:** Restore a personal record at a cost to someone else, protect another wrongly classified person, or refuse the official definition of identity.

**Strength:** Strong emotional stakes, vivid theme of a completely wrong person, haunting reinterpretation of the opening joke.

**Weakness:** Risks chosen-one energy and an overly metaphysical story. Keep the protagonist an ordinary accidental witness, not the unique magical anomaly.

**Initial creative hunch:** Pitch 1 provides the strongest plot motor; Pitch 2 provides a memorable world; Pitch 3 provides a possible *personal* consequence later, if earned.

## Candidate incident bank (brainstorming, not mandated chapters)

1. **The Restroom Authorization Corridor.** A pass permits the player to request an appointment to establish the right to use public facilities. Has narrative utility only if the escalation is brief and sharply funny; should not become endless bathroom counters.
2. **A Notice of Employment.** The player's denial is entered as proof of advanced discretion, increasing their clearance or obligation.
3. **The Demonstration That Complained.** A public machine files a procedural grievance for a reason nobody treats as impossible. The player chooses whether to treat it as a mechanical issue, labor issue or clerical misunderstanding.
4. **The Transport Schedule.** Destination and origin reports both look correct but cannot be reconciled. Travel and route choice provide more than conversational gameplay.
5. **The Specimen with Too Much History.** In a preserve, staff can document a creature's long past, although the institution containing it is new. Could be frightening or beautiful rather than a puzzle immediately solved.
6. **The Department of Previous Events.** A report describes an occurrence not yet recorded as happening. This could be satire, a red herring, or a route into a deeper mystery, not automatically proof of time travel.
7. **The Corridor Nobody Maintains.** Maintenance staff keep impossible equipment working through workarounds; a practical technician reveals more than an official researcher, without making science incompetent.
8. **The Mandatory Correction.** An innocent's outcome depends on whether the protagonist authorizes a routine discrepancy. A comic habit acquires ethical cost.
9. **The Public Ceremony.** Officially unveiling a system puts presentation, reality and human risk in the same scene. It is a candidate climax setting, not an ending lock.
10. **The Almost-Bathroom.** For once the objective seems within reach at a dramatically inappropriate moment. Avoid turning the entire world into a single repeated “not yet” punchline.

**Location variety principle:** Not every beat is a corridor, counter or NPC. Build set-pieces that include observation, movement by choice, machines, environment, weather, creatures, difficult physical actions, documents, and bystanders.

## Example five-stage emotional spine — not chapters yet

| Phase | Player motivation | Story promise | Possible reversal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Mundane | Find a bathroom | Strange city and normal-seeming inconveniences | An information request becomes appointment |
| 2. Unwanted | Cancel/fulfill misassignment | Unexpected access and absurd procedural jobs | Refusal creates more formal authority |
| 3. Expanding | Investigate or evade consequences | Varied travel and public-world incidents | Two trustworthy sources can't both be right |
| 4. Personal | Protect self/others; decide whom to trust | Consequences of a hidden error become human | Bureaucratic categories can cause actual harm |
| 5. Consequential | Decide what to do with partial understanding | Multiple plausible actions and costs | The simplest administrative act may affect many lives |

Each phase should still permit wit, beauty and small unrelated weirdness. There should be no required literal “chosen one” revelation or mandated secret mastermind.

## Character design questions, before naming or locking a cast

- Who is the first official who is **helpful** but cannot see why the procedure is absurd?
- Who knows the machinery hands-on (maintainer), and who is careful about what can actually be inferred (scientist)?
- Which recurring ordinary citizen gives the situation human consequences beyond paperwork?
- Who would legitimately disagree with stopping a useful but misunderstood technology?
- Who is funny because of deeply reasonable behavior inside unreasonable institutions—not because they are stupid?
- Can two players experience markedly different **relationships with the same character** based on what they did, not an arbitrary alignment score?

Old placeholders: receptionist Miss Voss, scientist Dr. Elias Rook, technician Marta Bell, director Havel and guide Penny Vale. These are names/roles from exploratory documents, not locked cast.

## First major creative questions

1. **What is the first true point of no easy return?** The protagonist can still try to leave. What makes continuing plausible instead of forced?
2. **How wide is the world?** One institution, exposition city, or eventual wilderness/off-world adventure? Scope may expand in story without requiring first-release production of all those places.
3. **What makes an institution act this way?** Tragic inherited safeguards, warped incentives, deliberate obfuscation or something else? “Bureaucracy for its own sake” should feel normal in-world, not arbitrary outside the writer's imagination.
4. **How personal do we get?** Is the central cost identity, harm to another person, society's dependence on technology, or an interplay?
5. **How much mystery should be knowable?** Let the author have coherent rules, but do not make the protagonist complete a compulsory evidence quiz.
6. **What is the bathroom payoff?** Recurring joke, ironic final-access moment, quiet character beat or deliberately unresolved? **Do not decide yet.**
7. **Are there unrelated mini-adventures?** Likely yes; ensure they develop world, relationships or mechanics even if not central-mystery clues.
